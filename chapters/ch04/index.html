    Chapter 4: Control Valves — Bodies, Trim, and Fisher Products

    Learning Objectives

    • Remember: Name the six main control valve body styles and identify their distinguishing characteristics
    • Understand: Explain how valve body and trim selection affects flow capacity, shutoff, noise, and rangeability
    • Apply: Match a valve body type to a given service condition (fluid type, pressure class, ΔP, temperature)
    • Analyze: Evaluate trade-offs between globe, ball, and butterfly valves for a specific application
    • Evaluate: Select appropriate Fisher valve models for common Applied Control customer scenarios

    4.1 What a Control Valve Does

    A control valve is a variable orifice. By changing the position of a movable element (plug, ball, disk) relative to a fixed seat, it changes the flow area — and therefore the flow rate — through the valve. The amount of flow at a given valve travel depends on three things:

    1. The pressure drop across the valve (ΔP = upstream pressure − downstream pressure)
    2. The fluid properties (density, viscosity, vapor pressure)
    3. The flow coefficient (Cv) of the valve at that travel position

    We'll cover Cv and sizing in Chapter 5. This chapter focuses on the physical hardware: body styles, trim configurations, and the Fisher product family.

    4.2 Globe Valves — The Control Valve Workhorse

    The globe valve is the most common control valve style for throttling service. Fluid enters one side of the body, passes through the seat port (the orifice), and exits the other side. A plug attached to the stem moves up and down to change the flow area.

    Why globe valves dominate throttling service:

    • Excellent throttling precision over a wide range of positions
    • Wide choice of trim styles (cage-guided, plug-guided, multi-stage)
    • Best shutoff capability (Class IV, V, VI achievable)
    • Long history of reliable performance in severe service

    Trade-offs: - Higher pressure drop in full-open position (compared to ball/butterfly) - Larger, heavier, and more expensive than quarter-turn alternatives - Not ideal for very large line sizes (>12") due to cost

    Globe Valve Configurations

    One seat ring, one plug. Simple and easy to maintain. Standard for most throttling applications. The Fisher ED and EZ valves are single-port globe valves.

    Best for: General liquid and gas service, moderate ΔP, where shutoff matters

    Two seat rings and a plug with two ports. The upward and downward flow forces partially cancel, reducing actuator force requirements for large valve sizes.

    Best for: Very large flow requirements where single-port actuator forces would be prohibitive; less common today with high-thrust pneumatic actuators

    Flow enters straight and exits at 90°. This allows the valve to function as an elbow, saving piping. Also better for flashing and erosive service — the impingement angle is more manageable.

    Best for: Flashing liquids, erosive slurries, where piping layout saves installation cost

    4.3 The Fisher Globe Valve Family

    Applied Control represents the Fisher brand — the world's most widely used control valve line. Here are the core Fisher globe valves you'll encounter:

    Fisher ED Control Valve

    The most versatile general-purpose control valve in the Fisher line.

    Attribute Specification
    Body style Single-port globe, cage-guided trim
    Size range ½" to 20"
    Pressure class ANSI 150 to 2500
    Temperature range –40°F to +450°F standard; extended with bonnet options
    Trim options Linear, equal-%, quick-opening, low-flow, anti-cavitation, noise attenuation
    Shutoff Class IV standard; Class V/VI available
    Typical service General liquid, gas, and steam; the default choice for most throttling applications

    The ED's cage-guided trim is a key advantage — the cage centers the plug throughout travel, giving consistent, stable control even at low travel percentages.

    Fisher EZ Control Valve

    A cost-optimized globe valve for lower-pressure general service.

    Attribute Specification
    Body style Single-port globe, stem-guided
    Size range ¾" to 6"
    Pressure class ANSI 150 to 300
    Typical service General utility service where a full-featured cage-guided trim is not required

    Fisher HP Series (High Pressure)

    For severe service: high pressure drop, high temperature, or demanding fluids.

    Attribute Specification
    Pressure class ANSI 600 to 4500
    Body design Heavier wall, reinforced body for extreme conditions
    Typical service Steam conditioning, high-pressure gas letdown, supercritical service

    Fisher Cavitrol III Trim

    Not a valve body — a trim option for Fisher ED and similar bodies that addresses cavitation in liquid service.

    • Multi-stage pressure drop: instead of dropping all the pressure at once (causing vapor bubbles to form and collapse violently), the Cavitrol III drops pressure in stages
    • Each stage drops pressure partially, keeping local pressure above vapor pressure throughout
    • Dramatically reduces noise, vibration, and erosion damage

    When to specify: Anytime the calculated sigma (cavitation index) falls below the valve's cavitation inception coefficient. Chapter 5 covers the calculation.

    Fisher Vee-Ball Valve

    A segmented ball valve with a V-notch characterized port. Not a globe valve — completely different flow geometry.

    • The V-notch gives an equal-percentage characteristic natively, without needing a shaped plug
    • Excellent for slurry, pulp stock, viscous liquids, and applications where solids pass-through is needed
    • Full line bore in the open position → low pressure drop, easy cleanability

    4.4 Ball Valves for Control Service

    Quarter-turn ball valves can throttle — but not all of them. A full-bore ball valve at mid-position is a poor throttling valve: most of its flow range is compressed into a small band of travel, and the uncharacterized port gives erratic gain across the range.

    For control service, use characterized ball valves:

    Segmented Ball Valve (V-Notch)

    • The Fisher Vee-Ball series
    • V-notch segment replaces full sphere — the notch creates a characterized opening
    • Better rangeability than a full-ball: typically 100:1 vs. 30:1 for a full-bore ball
    • Higher Cv for a given body size than globe valves → compact installations
    • Key market: Pulp and paper, mining, chemical slurries, large-flow liquid control

    Full-Bore Ball Valve

    • Best for on/off isolation; acceptable for slow-throttle applications with limited range requirements
    • Do not use for precise throttling where stable control at 20–60% open is needed
    • Fisher V150/V200 Vee-Ball valves cover control-service ball applications

    4.5 Butterfly Valves for Control

    Butterfly valves use a disk rotating around a central shaft. They are high Cv, low cost, and compact — but require careful selection for throttling.

    Concentric (Wafer) Butterfly

    • Disk and shaft share the same centerline
    • The seat engages the disk eccentrically → poor shutoff (typically Class II)
    • Not suitable for throttling service requiring stable control below ~20% open
    • Use: On/off isolation, large cooling water systems

    High-Performance Butterfly (HPBV)

    • Double-offset disk: the shaft is offset from both the disk centerline and the pipe centerline
    • Better shutoff (Class III–IV), better throttling range
    • Fisher 8580 HPBV series
    • Use: Large line sizes (8"–48"+) where cost of globe valve is prohibitive; utility services

    Triple-Offset Butterfly

    • Third offset: the seating surface is a cone, not a cylinder → zero friction on opening/closing (unlike single and double-offset where the seat drags across the disk)
    • Excellent shutoff (Class V/VI achievable), fire-safe designs, excellent for high-pressure/high-temperature
    • Fisher Vee-Ball V500 and related designs
    • Use: Critical isolation in refining, LNG, high-temperature steam

    4.6 Valve Body Materials

    Material selection is driven by fluid chemistry, temperature, and pressure class. Failure to specify correctly leads to corrosion, cracking, or contamination.

    Material Common spec Typical service
    Carbon steel (WCB) ASTM A216 Gr. WCB General service, hydrocarbons, steam to 750°F
    316 Stainless Steel (CF8M) ASTM A351 Gr. CF8M Corrosive liquids, food/pharma, aqueous solutions
    Alloy 20 (CN7M) ASTM A351 Gr. CN7M Sulfuric acid service, highly corrosive chemicals
    Duplex Stainless (CD4MCu) ASTM A890 Chloride environments, offshore, seawater
    Hastelloy C (CW6M) ASTM A494 Highly oxidizing acids, aggressive chemicals
    Bronze ASTM B62 Marine service, low-pressure water

    Chloride stress corrosion cracking

    Standard 316 SS can stress-crack in hot chloride environments (e.g., produced water in oil & gas). Always ask about chloride content and temperature before specifying stainless. Duplex or Hastelloy may be required.

    4.7 Pressure-Temperature (P-T) Ratings

    Every valve body has a maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP) at each operating temperature, determined by the body material and ANSI pressure class (ASME B16.34):

    ANSI Class Approx. max CWP at 100°F (WCB)
    Class 150 285 PSIG
    Class 300 740 PSIG
    Class 600 1480 PSIG
    Class 900 2220 PSIG
    Class 1500 3705 PSIG
    Class 2500 6170 PSIG

    Never derate from pressure alone

    Pressure rating drops with temperature. A Class 600 WCB valve rated at 1480 PSIG at 100°F is only rated at ~1050 PSIG at 800°F. Always check the P-T table for the actual operating temperature.

    4.8 End Connections

    How the valve attaches to the pipe matters for installation, removal, and maintenance:

    End connection Notes
    Flanged (RF or RTJ) Most common for process service; allows easy removal for maintenance
    Butt-weld (BW) Welded into the line; no flange leakage path; required for high-pressure Class 900+
    Socket-weld (SW) Small sizes (½"–2") in high-pressure service
    Screwed/Threaded (NPT) Small utility valves; avoid for process service where removal matters
    Wafer Butterfly valves sandwiched between flanges; compact but not suitable for high line forces

    4.9 Valve Leakage Classes (ANSI FCI 70-2)

    Not all valves shut off equally. The leakage class defines the maximum allowable leak at full close, critical for applications where even small leakage causes safety, environmental, or process problems.

    Class Max leakage Typical application
    Class I No test required On/off valves not requiring tight shutoff
    Class II 0.5% of rated Cv General throttling where leakage is acceptable
    Class III 0.1% of rated Cv Moderate leakage requirement
    Class IV 0.01% of rated Cv Standard for Fisher globe valves; most throttling service
    Class V 0.0005 mL/min/psi ΔP/in seat Tight shutoff; soft or lapped metal seats
    Class VI Bubble-tight (per test procedure) Cryogenic, toxic, critical isolation; requires soft seat

    Chapter Summary

    Control valves are variable orifices — the physical final element of every control loop. Globe valves dominate throttling service because of their precise controllability, wide trim options, and excellent shutoff capability. Fisher ED and EZ valves cover most general-purpose applications; Fisher HP serves severe service. Ball valves (Fisher Vee-Ball) compete for large-flow, slurry, and liquid applications. Butterfly valves shine in large line sizes where globe valve cost is prohibitive. Body material follows fluid chemistry; pressure class follows operating conditions. Leakage class specifies shutoff quality.

    Quiz

    Question 1 — Remember

    Which Fisher valve series uses a cage-guided, single-port globe body and is the most versatile general-purpose control valve in the Fisher line?

    • A) Fisher 8580
    • B) Fisher ED
    • C) Fisher Vee-Ball
    • D) Fisher HP Series

    Question 2 — Apply

    A customer needs a control valve for a 6" crude oil pipeline at Class 600 pressure rating, with 45% solids content (sand). Which valve type should you recommend first?

    • A) Fisher ED globe valve with cage-guided trim
    • B) Fisher Vee-Ball V-notch ball valve
    • C) High-performance butterfly valve
    • D) Fisher EZ globe valve

    Rationale: Full-bore V-notch ball allows solids pass-through; the globe valve would plug at the cage and trim.

    Question 3 — Analyze

    A 16" cooling water valve currently uses a concentric butterfly. The customer complains of poor control stability at 15–25% open. What is the root cause, and what would you recommend?

    • A) The valve is too large — recommend a smaller globe valve
    • B) The PID tuning is wrong — recommend retuning
    • C) Concentric butterflies have high-gain, unstable characteristics below ~20% open — recommend a high-performance double-offset butterfly or triple-offset for better throttling range
    • D) Add a volume booster to speed actuator response

    Sales Implications

    Sales bridge — Body and trim selection

    When a customer asks for a quote on a "control valve":

    1. Don't quote immediately. Get the data sheet first: fluid, pressure class, temperature, ΔP normal/max/shutoff, Cv required, shutoff class, any special service (cavitating? flashing? erosive? high noise?).
    2. Fisher's depth is a differentiator. A competitor offering a generic globe valve cannot match Fisher's trim library: Cavitrol III, WhisperFlo, characterized cages, low-flow trim. When service conditions are severe, Fisher trim options are often the only solution that avoids a special-order.
    3. Leakage class drives cost. Know upfront whether Class IV is acceptable or if Class V/VI is required — it changes body, trim, and actuator selection significantly.
    4. Ask about installed base. "What valves do you have in this service now? How are they performing?" opens valve diagnostic, replacement, and upgrade conversations.
