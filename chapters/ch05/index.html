    Chapter 5: Valve Sizing and Flow Coefficients

    Learning Objectives

    • Remember: State the definition of Cv and the basic liquid sizing equation
    • Understand: Explain why the same Cv can pass different flows depending on fluid properties and phase
    • Apply: Calculate required Cv for a liquid, gas, and steam service using IEC 60534 equations
    • Analyze: Determine whether a service condition requires anti-cavitation trim or noise attenuation
    • Evaluate: Select the appropriate Fisher valve model and trim for a given sized Cv and service severity

    5.1 The Flow Coefficient — Cv

    The flow coefficient (Cv) is the universal language of valve sizing. It is defined as:

    The number of US gallons per minute of water (60°F) that flows through a valve at a pressure drop of 1 PSI.

    $$C_v = q \sqrt{\frac{\rho / \rho_0}{\Delta P}}$$

    Where: - q = volumetric flow rate (GPM for liquid) - ρ/ρ₀ = specific gravity relative to water (dimensionless) - ΔP = pressure drop across fully open valve (PSID)

    Why Cv works: It captures the valve's physical geometry (seat size, travel, trim shape) in a single number that can be used across all fluids and pressures. Fisher publishes Cv tables for every travel position of every valve model, allowing accurate sizing.

    Cv is not Kv

    The European equivalent is Kv (flow in m³/h of water at 1 bar ΔP). Conversion: Cv = 1.156 × Kv. IEC documents use Kv; ISA documents use Cv. Fisher publishes both.

    5.2 Liquid Sizing — The Full IEC 60534 Equation

    For non-choked, turbulent liquid flow (the most common case):

    $$C_v = \frac{q}{N_1 F_P} \sqrt{\frac{G_f}{\Delta P}}$$

    For choked flow (when ΔP > ΔPchoked):

    $$C_v = \frac{q}{N_1 F_P F_L} \sqrt{\frac{G_f}{P_1 - F_F P_v}}$$

    Where:

    Symbol Description Typical value
    N₁ Numerical constant (depends on units) 0.0865 (m³/h, bar) or 1.0 (GPM, PSI)
    FP Piping geometry factor (reducers, etc.) 0.95–1.0
    FL Liquid pressure recovery factor 0.85–0.95 for globe
    Gf Specific gravity of liquid (water = 1.0) Process-dependent
    FF Critical pressure ratio factor ≈ 0.96 − 0.28√(Pᵥ/Pc)

    Practical liquid sizing procedure:

    1. Get: flow rate, inlet pressure, outlet pressure, fluid, temperature
    2. Look up or calculate: Gf, Pᵥ at temperature, Pc (if cavitation possible)
    3. Check if ΔP > ΔPchoked (choked condition); use appropriate equation
    4. Solve for required Cv
    5. Select valve with installed Cv ≥ required Cv at desired travel (typically 70–80% open)
    6. Verify: rangeability, installed characteristic, leakage class

    5.3 Gas and Vapor Sizing

    Gases and vapors are compressible — density changes with pressure. IEC 60534 uses the expansion factor Y to account for this.

    For non-choked gas flow:

    $$C_v = \frac{q}{N_9 F_P P_1 Y} \sqrt{\frac{M T_1 Z}{x}}$$

    For choked gas flow (when x ≥ Fγ xT):

    $$C_v = \frac{q}{N_9 F_P P_1 \cdot 0.667} \sqrt{\frac{M T_1 Z}{F_\gamma x_T}}$$

    Where:

    Symbol Description
    x Pressure drop ratio = ΔP/P₁
    Y Expansion factor = 1 − x/(3Fγ xT)
    xT Pressure drop ratio at choked flow (valve-specific)
    Fγ Ratio of specific heats factor = γ/1.4
    M Molecular weight of gas
    T₁ Inlet temperature (K)
    Z Compressibility factor (1.0 for ideal gas)
    N₉ Numerical constant (units-dependent)

    Gas sizing: always check for choked flow

    For high-pressure gas letdown (regulators, pressure control valves on gas lines), choked flow is common. If you use the non-choked equation in a choked condition, you will specify a valve that is too small.

    5.4 Steam Sizing

    Steam sizing depends on whether the steam is superheated (treat as gas) or saturated (use steam tables):

    Superheated steam (same form as gas, using specific volume from steam tables):

    $$C_v = \frac{W}{N_6 F_P Y \sqrt{x P_1 \rho_1}}$$

    Where: - W = mass flow rate (kg/h or lb/h) - N₆ = numerical constant - ρ₁ = density at inlet (from steam tables, kg/m³)

    Saturated steam: Must use steam table properties at the saturation line. Never assume ideal gas for saturated steam.

    5.5 Flow Characteristics — Installed vs. Inherent

    The flow characteristic describes how Cv changes with valve travel. It's not how much flow passes — it's the shape of the curve.

    Inherent Characteristics

    Characteristic Cv formula Shape
    Linear Cv = Cv_max × t Straight line
    Equal-percentage Cv = Cv_max × Rⁱ⁻¹ Exponential (R = rangeability, typically 50)
    Quick-opening Cv = Cv_max × √t Square-root curve

    Where t = travel as a fraction (0 to 1).

    Equal-percentage is the most common choice for throttling service. It provides constant process gain when the valve is the dominant pressure drop in the system.

    Linear is correct when the valve pressure drop is constant fraction of system ΔP.

    Quick-opening is for on/off service where a large flow area is needed immediately on opening (safety relief bypass, on/off control).

    Installed Characteristics

    The installed characteristic is what the valve actually does in the piping system, which differs from the inherent characteristic because system ΔP changes with flow. The distortion is captured by the valve authority (β):

    $$\beta = \frac{\Delta P_{valve, full flow}}{\Delta P_{system, full flow}}$$

    • β > 0.25: installed ≈ inherent (good control)
    • β < 0.1: severe distortion; consider linear trim or resizing

    MicroSim: Compare inherent flow characteristics and see how system authority distorts the installed curve.

    5.6 Rangeability

    Rangeability is the ratio of maximum to minimum controllable Cv:

    $$R = \frac{Cv_{max}}{Cv_{min}}$$

    Valve type Typical rangeability
    Globe (cage-guided, Fisher ED) 50:1
    Globe (stem-guided, Fisher EZ) 30:1
    V-notch ball (Fisher Vee-Ball) 100:1
    Butterfly (concentric) 10:1
    High-performance butterfly 20:1

    Low rangeability means the valve cannot control well at low flow rates — it jumps from closed to significant flow with small stem movement. This causes hunting and instability at low loads.

    5.7 Sizing for Cavitation and Noise

    Anti-Cavitation Sizing

    When calculated σ < σ_i (inception sigma from Fisher catalog), specify Cavitrol III trim:

    1. The multi-stage trim drops pressure in steps: each stage drops pressure by a small amount
    2. After each stage, pressure recovers slightly before the next drop
    3. At no point does local pressure fall below vapor pressure
    4. The Fisher Cavitrol III is available in 2-stage (moderate) and 3-stage (severe) configurations

    Sizing impact: Cavitrol III has lower Cv than standard trim for the same body size. Size the valve body assuming Cavitrol III Cv, then select body size.

    Noise Calculation and WhisperFlo Selection

    IEC 60534-8-3 (aerodynamic noise) and IEC 60534-8-4 (hydrodynamic noise) provide standardized methods. The result is sound pressure level (dB) at 1 meter downstream.

    If calculated noise > 85 dBA: specify WhisperFlo or WhisperTrim If calculated noise > 95 dBA: consider multi-stage trim or staged pressure reduction

    Fisher Whisper noise attenuation trims: Multiple small orifices break a single high-velocity jet into many small jets. Noise ∝ velocity⁶; reducing jet velocity by half cuts noise by 18 dB.

    5.9 Sizing Example — Worked Problem

    Service: Cooling water - Flow: 200 GPM at normal; 250 GPM maximum - Inlet pressure: 80 PSIG - Outlet pressure: 45 PSIG (normal), 40 PSIG (maximum) - Fluid: Water at 70°F (Gf = 1.0, Pᵥ = 0.36 PSIA) - Required leakage: Class IV

    Step 1 — Normal Cv:

    $$C_v = \frac{200}{1.0} \sqrt{\frac{1.0}{80 - 45}} = 200 \times \sqrt{\frac{1}{35}} = 200 \times 0.169 = 33.8$$

    Step 2 — Maximum Cv:

    $$C_v = \frac{250}{1.0} \sqrt{\frac{1.0}{80 - 40}} = 250 \times \sqrt{\frac{1}{40}} = 250 \times 0.158 = 39.5$$

    Step 3 — Check cavitation:

    $$\sigma = \frac{P_2 - P_v}{\Delta P} = \frac{45 - 0.36}{35} = \frac{44.64}{35} = 1.28$$

    For a Fisher ED, σᵢ ≈ 0.8 — since σ = 1.28 > σᵢ, no cavitation expected.

    Step 4 — Select valve:

    Required Cv_max = 39.5. Fisher ED 3" Class 150 has rated Cv = 68 at full open. At 70% travel, Cv ≈ 43. → Select 3" Fisher ED, Class 150, equal-percentage trim, Class IV shutoff.

    Chapter Summary

    Valve sizing is the process of matching a valve's Cv to the process requirements. Cv is the universal sizing coefficient: GPM of water at 1 PSI ΔP. IEC 60534 equations extend this to liquids (with viscosity and cavitation corrections), gases (with expansion factor Y and molecular weight), and steam (using steam table properties). Flow characteristics — linear, equal-percentage, quick-opening — determine how Cv changes with travel; installed characteristics depend on system authority. Rangeability defines the controllable flow range. When service conditions indicate cavitation, specify Cavitrol III; for noise, specify WhisperFlo. Fisher's Valvelink software automates these calculations — the engineer's role is to understand the inputs and interpret the output.

    Quiz

    Question 1 — Remember

    Cv is defined as the number of US GPM of water that flows through a valve at what pressure drop?

    • A) 5 PSID
    • B) 1 PSID
    • C) 10 PSID
    • D) 0.1 PSID

    Question 2 — Apply

    A gas application has P₁ = 100 PSIG, P₂ = 40 PSIG, so x = 60/114.7 = 0.523. If xT for the valve is 0.64 and Fγ = 1.0, is the flow choked?

    • A) Yes — because P₂ < P₁
    • B) No — because x (0.523) < Fγ × xT (0.64), so flow is not choked yet
    • C) Yes — because x > 0.5 for any gas
    • D) Cannot determine without fluid molecular weight

    Question 3 — Evaluate

    A customer is sizing a valve for hot water at 180°C. Vapor pressure at that temperature is 10.0 bar abs. Upstream pressure is 18 bar, downstream is 6 bar. What do you recommend?

    • A) Standard Fisher ED with equal-percentage trim
    • B) Check sigma: σ = (6 - 10)/12 = −0.33 < 0 — this is FLASHING, not cavitation. Specify angle body valve with hard trim (Stellite/ceramic) directed to handle two-phase flow, or multi-stage pressure reduction upstream.
    • C) Fisher Cavitrol III trim is sufficient
    • D) High-performance butterfly valve

    Sales Implications

    Sales bridge — Sizing to specification

    1. Offer to size it for them. "Send me your data sheet and I'll run it through Valvelink." This positions Applied Control as a partner, not a catalog order desk. A customer who depends on your sizing expertise is a loyal customer.
    2. Document severe service in writing. When your sizing calculation shows cavitation or excessive noise, put it in the quote. If the customer chooses standard trim anyway to save money and the valve fails, you have documented that you recommended the correct solution.
    3. Show the cost of undersizing vs. oversizing. An undersized valve can't make rate — production loss. An oversized valve operates near closed — poor control, stem wear, packing leakage. Correct sizing is not just technical — it's an economic argument.
    4. Rangeability as a differentiator: Fisher ED's 50:1 rangeability vs. a competitor's 20:1 for the same body size is a specific, auditable advantage. Translate it: "Your minimum controllable flow is 2% vs. 5% of rated — that's 2.5× better turndown."
