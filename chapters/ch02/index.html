    Chapter 2: Fluid Mechanics for Control Valves

    Learning Objectives

    • Remember: State Bernoulli's equation and define the four fluid flow regimes relevant to valve sizing
    • Understand: Explain how pressure drop across a valve creates flow, and why cavitation occurs in liquid service
    • Apply: Calculate Reynolds number and determine whether flow is turbulent or laminar for a given service condition
    • Analyze: Predict when flashing and cavitation will occur using vapor pressure and the cavitation index (sigma)
    • Evaluate: Select the correct Fisher trim option (standard, Cavitrol III, WhisperFlo) based on calculated fluid conditions

    2.1 Why Fluid Mechanics Matters to a Valve Salesperson

    Every control valve application involves fluid moving through a restriction. Whether you're sizing a Fisher ED for cooling water or specifying a Cavitrol III trim for a de-superheating station, you are applying fluid mechanics — whether you realize it or not.

    The physics determines:

    • How much flow a valve passes (Cv, covered in Chapter 5)
    • Whether the fluid will cavitate, erode the trim, and destroy the valve in months
    • How much noise the valve will generate (regulatory and structural concern)
    • Whether special trim is required — and how to justify the premium price

    Understanding fluid mechanics is what separates a parts-order salesperson from a trusted technical advisor.

    2.2 The Bernoulli Equation — Energy Conservation in Flow

    Bernoulli's equation is the foundation of all valve flow theory. It states that in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, the total energy per unit volume is constant:

    $$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + \rho g z_2$$

    For valves, elevation is usually negligible, so:

    $$P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2$$

    What this means for a valve: As fluid accelerates through the seat port (vena contracta — the narrowest point of flow), pressure drops. At the vena contracta, velocity is maximum and pressure is minimum. After the vena contracta, flow decelerates and pressure partially recovers.

    The Vena Contracta

    The vena contracta is the critical point in valve sizing. Pressure at the vena contracta can fall below vapor pressure even when downstream pressure is above vapor pressure. This is where cavitation begins — inside the valve, not downstream.

    2.3 Reynolds Number — Turbulent vs. Laminar Flow

    The Reynolds number (Re) classifies flow behavior:

    $$Re = \frac{\rho v D}{\mu} = \frac{v D}{\nu}$$

    Where: - ρ = fluid density (kg/m³) - v = mean flow velocity (m/s) - D = pipe diameter (m) - μ = dynamic viscosity (Pa·s) - ν = kinematic viscosity (m²/s)

    Re Range Flow Regime Effect on Valve
    Re < 2,000 Laminar Cv drops; turbulent Cv tables are invalid; flow is highly viscosity-dependent
    2,000–4,000 Transitional Unpredictable; avoid if possible
    Re > 4,000 Turbulent Standard Cv equations apply; most control valve service

    Viscous service is a sizing trap

    High-viscosity fluids (heavy crude, molasses, polymer solutions) can easily be in laminar or transitional flow at normal velocities. If you size a valve using standard Cv tables for a viscous fluid, the valve will be undersized — the customer will blame it for not making flow. Always check Reynolds number for viscosity > 20 cP.

    IEC 60534 and ISA 75.01 include viscosity correction factors (Fp) for this reason.

    2.4 Pressure Drop and the Pressure Profile Through a Valve

    Understanding how pressure varies through a valve — not just across it — is essential.

    Pressure Profile Through a Globe Valve

P1 (upstream) ───────┐
                      │  ← Pressure recovery (partial)
                      │
                      ├──── Pvc (vena contracta, minimum pressure)
                      │
                      └──── P2 (downstream / back pressure)

Key relationships:
• ΔP = P1 - P2     (overall pressure drop)
• ΔPvc = P1 - Pvc  (drop to vena contracta)
• Pvc < P2         (vena contracta pressure is always lower than P2)

    The pressure recovery factor (FL) characterizes how much pressure recovers from vena contracta to outlet:

    $$F_L^2 = \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta P_{vc}} = \frac{P_1 - P_2}{P_1 - P_{vc}}$$

    Higher FL means less recovery — the vena contracta pressure is closer to downstream pressure. Globe valves have higher FL (0.85–0.95) than ball valves (0.55–0.75), which means globe valves are less prone to cavitation for a given service condition.

    2.5 Cavitation — The Valve Killer

    Cavitation occurs when local pressure drops below the fluid's vapor pressure, forming vapor bubbles. When these bubbles collapse (implode) as pressure recovers downstream of the vena contracta, they release intense shock waves — enough to pit hardened stainless steel.

    Cavitation Stages

    1. Cavitation inception: First bubble formation. Slight noise, no damage yet.
    2. Developed cavitation: Sustained bubble formation and collapse. Crackling/popping sound like gravel in the pipe. Metal erosion begins.
    3. Choked flow: Vapor bubbles fill the vena contracta, limiting flow regardless of how much ΔP increases. Flow is determined by upstream conditions only.

    The Cavitation Index (Sigma)

    The cavitation index (σ) quantifies the risk:

    $$\sigma = \frac{P_2 - P_v}{\Delta P}$$

    Where: - P₂ = downstream absolute pressure - Pᵥ = fluid vapor pressure at operating temperature - ΔP = P₁ − P₂

    Interpretation: - σ >> σᵢ (inception sigma): Safe — no cavitation - σ ≈ σᵢ: Approaching cavitation — consider anti-cavitation trim - σ < σᵢ: Cavitation is occurring — anti-cavitation or multi-stage ΔP required - σ → 0: Approaching choked flow

    Each valve style and trim has its own critical sigma value. Fisher publishes σᵢ for each trim option.

    MicroSim: Adjust upstream pressure and downstream back-pressure to visualize cavitation onset and the pressure profile through the valve vena contracta.

    2.6 Flashing — When Cavitation Doesn't Recover

    Flashing occurs when downstream pressure is also below vapor pressure. Unlike cavitation, the vapor bubbles don't collapse — they remain as vapor, and the flow exits the valve as a two-phase mixture.

    Effects of flashing: - Dramatic increase in fluid velocity (vapor occupies ~1,600× the volume of liquid water) - Severe erosion from high-velocity two-phase flow - Reduced Cv (two-phase flow requires different sizing equations) - Noise and vibration

    Where it occurs: Hot condensate, boiler feedwater recirculation, pressure letdown above saturation temperature.

    Mitigation: - Angle body valves (Fisher A31A): direct the high-velocity two-phase flow away from critical surfaces - Hard trim materials: Stellite or ceramic seat/plug faces resist erosion - Low-recovery valve bodies: some globe designs intentionally prevent pressure recovery below downstream Pv

    Flashing vs. cavitation — the quick test

    If P₂ > Pᵥ → cavitation possible (bubbles collapse) If P₂ < Pᵥ → flashing (bubbles persist into downstream pipe)

    2.7 Choked Flow — Maximum Flow Rate

    Choked flow is the flow rate at which increasing ΔP produces no additional flow. It occurs when the vena contracta velocity reaches the local speed of sound in the fluid.

    Liquid Choked Flow

    $$q_{max} = N_1 F_L C_v \sqrt{\frac{P_1 - F_F P_v}{\rho / \rho_0}}$$

    Where FF is the liquid critical pressure ratio factor:

    $$F_F = 0.96 - 0.28\sqrt{\frac{P_v}{P_c}}$$

    Pc is the thermodynamic critical pressure of the fluid.

    What this means practically: Beyond choked flow, the valve cannot deliver more flow no matter how you open it. If a customer says "the valve is wide open but we can't make rate," check whether the process is in choked flow before condemning the valve.

    Gas/Vapor Choked Flow

    For compressible fluids (gases, steam), choked flow occurs at a critical pressure ratio:

    $$\frac{P_2}{P_1} \leq \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$$

    For air (γ = 1.4), this gives a critical ratio of ~0.528. When P₂/P₁ < 0.528, flow is choked and independent of downstream pressure.

    2.8 Noise — Hydrodynamic and Aerodynamic

    Control valve noise is a frequent customer complaint — regulatory, structural, and operator safety concerns.

    Hydrodynamic Noise (Liquid Service)

    • Primary source: cavitation bubble collapse
    • Spectrum peaks at 5–10 kHz
    • Fisher solution: Cavitrol III anti-cavitation trim — drops pressure in stages, preventing bubble formation

    Aerodynamic Noise (Gas/Steam Service)

    • Primary source: jet mixing at vena contracta — turbulent shear generates broadband noise
    • Spectrum peaks at 1–8 kHz
    • Fisher solutions: WhisperFlo and WhisperTrim — multiple small orifices spread the ΔP across many small jets, dramatically reducing noise per IEC 60534-8-3

    Calculating noise — the IEC method

    IEC 60534-8-4 gives a standardized method for predicting valve noise. The calculation requires: Cv, FL, ΔP, fluid properties, pipe size, and pipe wall thickness.

    Fisher's Valvelink software performs this calculation automatically. A valve producing more than 85 dBA at 1 meter requires special consideration for worker safety (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.95).

    2.9 Compressible Flow — Gas and Steam Sizing

    Gases and steam require a different approach because density changes with pressure. Key differences from liquid sizing:

    Expansion factor (Y): Accounts for the fact that gas density decreases as it expands through the valve:

    $$Y = 1 - \frac{x}{3 F_\gamma x_T}$$

    Where: - x = ΔP/P₁ (pressure drop ratio) - xT = pressure drop ratio at choked flow (valve characteristic) - Fγ = ratio of specific heats factor (γ/1.4)

    For steam: Use steam tables for density and enthalpy. Superheated steam is treated like an ideal gas with corrections; saturated steam requires special handling to account for the wet/dry boundary.

    2.10 Key Fluid Properties for Valve Sizing

    Property Symbol Units Where it matters
    Density ρ kg/m³ All Cv calculations
    Dynamic viscosity μ Pa·s Reynolds number, viscosity correction
    Kinematic viscosity ν m²/s Re = vD/ν
    Vapor pressure Pᵥ bar absolute Cavitation, flashing
    Critical pressure Pc bar absolute FF factor calculation
    Specific heat ratio γ — Gas/steam sizing, xT
    Compressibility factor Z — Real-gas corrections at high pressure

    Where to find fluid properties

    • Perry's Chemical Engineers' Handbook: Comprehensive physical property tables
    • NIST WebBook (webbook.nist.gov): Free online fluid properties database
    • Fisher Valvelink / Emerson Valve Wizard: Pulls properties automatically from REFPROP for common fluids
    • Steam tables: IFC-67 or IAPWS-IF97; built into all valve sizing software

    Chapter Summary

    Fluid mechanics governs what happens inside every control valve. Bernoulli's equation shows that pressure drops at the vena contracta — the narrowest point — can fall below vapor pressure, triggering cavitation (if P₂ > Pᵥ) or flashing (if P₂ < Pᵥ). The cavitation index (σ) quantifies risk; values below the inception sigma require anti-cavitation trim. Choked flow limits maximum throughput regardless of ΔP. Gas and steam service require expansion factor (Y) corrections. Reynolds number determines whether viscous corrections are needed. These calculations drive the specification of Fisher trim — Cavitrol III for cavitation, WhisperFlo/WhisperTrim for noise — and are the technical basis for commanding premium prices on severe service solutions.

    Quiz

    Question 1 — Remember

    At the vena contracta, what happens to fluid velocity and pressure compared to upstream conditions?

    • A) Velocity decreases; pressure increases
    • B) Velocity increases; pressure decreases
    • C) Velocity and pressure both decrease
    • D) Velocity and pressure are unchanged

    Question 2 — Understand

    A customer is experiencing crackling/gravel noise in a control valve handling hot water at 160°C. The downstream pressure is 4 bar. The vapor pressure of water at 160°C is 6.2 bar. What is happening, and what is likely needed?

    • A) Aerodynamic noise from high gas velocity — specify WhisperFlo trim
    • B) Flashing — downstream pressure is below vapor pressure; specify an angle body with hard trim (Stellite) or direct the two-phase flow path away from seat
    • C) Cavitation — downstream pressure is above vapor pressure; specify Cavitrol III
    • D) Normal turbulence — no action needed

    Rationale: P₂ = 4 bar < Pᵥ = 6.2 bar → flashing, not cavitation.

    Question 3 — Apply

    Flow through a control valve has stopped increasing despite increasing ΔP. The fluid is liquid water with P₂ = 3.5 bar and Pᵥ = 2.3 bar. What most likely explains this behavior?

    • A) The valve is undersized — increase Cv
    • B) Pipe friction losses are dominating
    • C) Choked flow — the valve has reached maximum flow for current conditions; increasing ΔP beyond the choked condition produces no more flow
    • D) The valve has failed mechanically

    Sales Implications

    Sales bridge — Fluid mechanics to Fisher severe service

    Use cavitation and flashing as the opening for premium trim conversations:

    1. Ask: "What fluid, pressure, and temperature?" → If hot liquid or significant pressure drop, always calculate σ.
    2. Compute or estimate σ: If customer's ΔP is large relative to (P₂ − Pᵥ), cavitation is likely. Show them the math — it's persuasive and positions you as the expert.
    3. Quote Cavitrol III proactively: The trim premium is modest; the cost of cavitation damage (unplanned shutdowns, re-trim, replacement) is enormous. Frame it as insurance.
    4. Noise is a safety and regulatory trigger: If your calculation suggests noise > 85 dBA, mention OSHA 1910.95. Plant safety officers respond. This is a conversation engineering management has to join.
    5. "What happened to the last valve in this service?" is the most powerful question you can ask in severe service. The answer almost always justifies special trim — and Applied Control's repair shop can analyze the failed trim and confirm the diagnosis.
