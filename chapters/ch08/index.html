    Chapter 8: Steam Systems & Traps

    Learning Objectives

    • Remember: Identify the three phases of steam (saturated, superheated, wet) and state the relationship between steam pressure and saturation temperature
    • Understand: Explain why condensate must be removed from steam systems and what happens when condensate accumulates in steam piping
    • Apply: Select the correct steam trap type for a given steam system application (drip leg, tracer, heat exchanger, turbine drain)
    • Analyze: Diagnose a failed-open vs. failed-closed steam trap from temperature and condensate return measurements
    • Evaluate: Justify a steam trap survey and repair program using energy cost savings calculations

    8.1 Steam System Fundamentals

    Steam is one of the most common process utilities — used for heating, tracing, stripping, turbine drive, and direct injection. Understanding steam physics is essential for correct valve selection, sizing, and troubleshooting.

    Steam Phases

    Phase Description Key characteristics
    Saturated steam Steam in equilibrium with liquid water at the same pressure Temperature and pressure are linked by steam tables; contains no superheat
    Superheated steam Steam heated above saturation temperature at the same pressure Temperature > saturation temperature; used for turbines and process heating
    Wet steam Mixture of saturated steam and water droplets Quality < 1.0; droplets cause erosion; must be dried before turbine admission

    Pressure-Temperature Relationship

    Saturated steam temperature is determined entirely by pressure — from the steam tables:

    Pressure (PSIG) Saturation temperature (°F) Latent heat (BTU/lb)
    15 250 945
    50 298 912
    100 338 889
    150 366 857
    250 406 820
    600 490 731

    Higher pressure steam carries more energy per pound but at higher temperature. Process heating typically uses 15–150 PSIG; turbine steam is often 600–1500 PSIG superheated.

    Condensate Formation

    As steam gives up its latent heat to the process, it condenses to liquid water (condensate). This condensate must be removed continuously because:

    1. Water hammer: High-velocity steam sweeping condensate creates destructive pressure surges — "water hammer" — that break pipe joints, fittings, and valve bodies
    2. Loss of heat transfer area: Condensate pooling in heat exchangers reduces effective steam surface area
    3. Corrosion: Oxygen in condensate causes pitting corrosion; dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid
    4. Turbine blade erosion: Wet steam or condensate entering a steam turbine erodes blades catastrophically

    8.2 Steam Traps

    A steam trap is an automatic valve that removes condensate and non-condensable gases from steam systems while preventing live steam from passing through. The trap must distinguish between condensate (which it passes) and steam (which it blocks).

    The Three Steam Trap Operating Principles

    1. Mechanical traps — use the density difference between steam and condensate:

    • Float and thermostatic (F&T): A float rises with condensate level, opening a discharge valve. A thermostatic element handles air and CO₂. Best for large condensate loads and heat exchangers.
    • Inverted bucket: A bucket inverted in the trap body — steam keeps the bucket floating (trap closed); condensate fills the bucket, sinking it (trap opens to discharge). Best for high-pressure steam with light condensate loads.

    2. Thermodynamic traps — use the difference in thermodynamic properties between steam and condensate:

    • Disc trap (thermodynamic): A disc lifts under condensate (low velocity) and closes under steam (high velocity creates low pressure under disc, holding it shut). Simple, compact, and rugged. Best for tracer lines and drip legs. Susceptible to failure in cold/freezing conditions.

    3. Thermostatic traps — use the temperature difference between steam and subcooled condensate:

    • Bimetallic: A bimetallic element expands with temperature, closing the discharge port when near steam temperature; opens when condensate cools. Best for tracing, jacketed pipes, and low-pressure heating.
    • Balanced pressure: A capsule filled with a fluid that vaporizes at steam temperature. Capsule expands to close the trap on steam; condenses below steam temperature, opening the trap. Very sensitive to condensate subcooling.

    Steam Trap Selection Guide

    Application Preferred trap type Why
    Drip legs (main steam lines) Inverted bucket or disc Handles start-up loads; rugged; compact
    Tracer lines Disc or bimetallic Small condensate load; freeze-resistant bimetallic preferred in cold climates
    Heat exchangers (modulating) Float and thermostatic Handles variable condensate load; continuous discharge
    Turbine drains Inverted bucket or float Critical service; must pass condensate without any steam loss
    Jacketed pipes/reactors Balanced pressure or bimetallic Uniform heating; subcooling prevents steam waste

    8.3 Steam Trap Failure Modes

    Steam traps fail in two directions — each has different consequences and detection methods:

    Failed Open (Steam Blowing Through)

    A failed-open trap passes live steam into the condensate return system, wasting energy.

    Symptoms: - Condensate return temperature unusually high downstream of trap - Hissing sound at trap; visual steam plume at discharge (if vented to atmosphere) - Condensate return system overpressured - Higher-than-expected steam consumption with no process change

    Detection: - Ultrasonic testing: A failed-open trap produces high-frequency noise from steam flow vs. intermittent condensate discharge sounds - Infrared thermometry: Downstream temperature nearly equal to upstream = steam blowing through - Visual inspection: Steam (not water) at discharge point

    Energy cost of a failed-open trap:

    A ½" disc trap failing open at 150 PSIG loses approximately 50 lb/hr of steam. At $8/1000 lb steam, that is $3,500/year of wasted energy from a single trap. A plant with 500 traps and 10% failure rate loses $175,000/year.

    Failed Closed (Condensate Backing Up)

    A failed-closed trap blocks condensate drainage — condensate backs up into the steam system.

    Symptoms: - Heat exchanger losing capacity (condensate flooding tubes) - Water hammer in steam lines (condensate slug hitting valves and fittings) - Process temperature cannot reach setpoint - Downstream steam temperature lower than expected

    Detection: - Upstream temperature high (steam pressure normal); downstream cold = blocked trap - No discharge activity at trap outlet - Ultrasonic: silence at a trap that should be discharging

    8.4 Steam Control Valves

    Steam service is one of the most demanding for control valves because of high temperatures, high pressures, high differential pressures, and the phase-change behavior of steam.

    Key Steam Valve Selection Criteria

    Temperature: Steam at 600 PSIG is saturated at 490°F; superheated steam for turbine service may reach 900–1050°F. Valve body, bonnet, and packing materials must be rated for service temperature:

    Temperature range Body material Packing
    Up to 450°F WCB carbon steel PTFE rings
    450–750°F WC6 chrome-moly Graphite rings
    750–1050°F WC9 chrome-moly Graphite + high-temp rings

    Pressure drop and noise: High-pressure-drop steam produces intense aerodynamic noise. The Fisher WhisperFlo and Whisper III trim designs use staged pressure reduction to keep velocities subsonic, limiting noise below 85 dB(A) at pipe wall.

    Condensing steam and water hammer: A steam control valve that closes during periods of condensate accumulation can experience water hammer when it reopens — a slug of liquid is accelerated by the incoming steam. The Fisher EHD (extra heavy duty) steam conditioning valve is designed for direct injection of steam into process streams, handling the condensation shock loads and desuperheating requirements simultaneously.

    Fisher 657 Actuator on Steam Valves

    Steam control valves typically use the Fisher 657 (spring-to-open, fail-open) or 667 (spring-to-close, fail-closed) pneumatic actuator. For steam to reboilers and heating services, fail-closed (667) is standard — loss of instrument air stops steam flow rather than flooding the heat exchanger. For steam turbine bypass, fail-open may be required to maintain steam flow to the condenser.

    8.5 Fisher 627 Regulator — Self-Contained Pressure Control

    For steam distribution headers and utility steam that does not require DCS control, the Fisher 627 pressure-reducing regulator provides self-contained pressure control without a controller, positioner, or instrument air supply:

    • Operation: Downstream pressure acts through a sensing line on a diaphragm that actuates the valve plug directly. The spring sets the target downstream pressure.
    • Accuracy: ±3–5% of set point (adequate for utility steam distribution)
    • No external power required: Fully self-contained; works during power and instrument air failures
    • Range: Set points from 2 PSIG to 400 PSIG; inlet pressure up to 600 PSIG

    When to use the 627: - Utility steam let-down stations feeding non-critical heating loads - Remote locations without instrument air - Building heating systems, tank heating, winterization - Backup pressure control where DCS failure should not interrupt steam

    When NOT to use the 627: - Tight pressure regulation required (±1% or better) - DCS feedback and logging required - Variable demand requiring responsive control (use DVC6200-equipped control valve)

    Chapter Summary

    Steam systems require understanding of phase behavior — the saturation temperature at any pressure is fixed by thermodynamics, and condensate formation is inevitable. Steam traps automatically remove condensate while blocking live steam. The three trap operating principles (mechanical, thermodynamic, thermostatic) each suit different applications: float-and-thermostatic for heat exchangers, disc traps for drip legs, bimetallic for tracers. Failed-open traps waste energy; failed-closed traps cause water hammer and loss of heat transfer. Steam control valves face extreme temperature and pressure conditions requiring appropriate body materials, graphite packing, and noise-attenuating trim. The Fisher 627 regulator provides self-contained pressure reduction for non-critical utility steam without any external power or air supply.

    Question 2 — Understand

    A heat exchanger on a 100 PSIG steam system is losing heating capacity. The steam pressure is normal. An infrared scan shows the steam trap downstream is cold. What is the most likely diagnosis?

    • A) The control valve is closed
    • B) The steam trap has failed closed — condensate is backing up into the heat exchanger, flooding the steam-side tubes and reducing the effective heat transfer area
    • C) The trap has failed open — steam is blowing through
    • D) The steam supply pressure is too low

ED+DVC6200 if: tight pressure regulation needed, DCS logging required, load varies significantly, or the steam feeds processes requiring precise temperature control
    • B) Always use the control valve — it's more accurate
    • C) Always use the 627 — it's cheaper
