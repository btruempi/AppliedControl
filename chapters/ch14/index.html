    Chapter 14: Asset Reliability & Valve Diagnostics

    Learning Objectives

    • Remember: Define the four maintenance strategies (reactive, preventive, predictive, reliability-centered) and state which produces the lowest total cost
    • Understand: Explain how AMS Device Manager converts HART diagnostic data from field devices into actionable maintenance work orders
    • Apply: Interpret a DVC6200 friction trend showing 300% increase over 12 months and determine the appropriate maintenance action and timing
    • Analyze: Build a reliability ROI calculation showing the net present value of a DVC6200 diagnostic program for a plant with 75 critical control valves
    • Evaluate: Design an Applied Control valve health program (baseline, monitoring, response) for a new customer with 200 Fisher control valves

    14.1 The Reliability Imperative

    Unplanned downtime is the most expensive event in a process plant. Industry benchmarks:

    Industry Cost per hour of unplanned downtime
    Oil refinery $50,000–$200,000
    Chemical plant $20,000–$100,000
    Power generation $30,000–$150,000
    Pharmaceutical $100,000–$500,000
    Pulp and paper $15,000–$50,000

    Control valves are the #1 cause of process-related downtime — more shutdowns trace to valve failures than any other single component type. Yet most plants still practice reactive maintenance — they fix valves after they fail.

    14.2 Maintenance Strategy Hierarchy

    Reactive Maintenance (Run-to-Failure)

    The valve is not serviced until it fails or causes a process problem.

    Cost structure: Low routine cost, very high corrective cost. Emergency repair includes: unplanned labor (overtime), expedited parts, lost production, potential safety incident.

    When appropriate: For non-critical valves with no production impact and low repair cost (utilities, bypasses, low-cost isolations).

    Preventive Maintenance (Time-Based)

    Valves are serviced on a fixed schedule regardless of actual condition — for example, every 2 years pull and inspect all critical valves.

    Problem: Over-maintains healthy valves (wastes labor) while under-maintaining deteriorating valves that fail between PM intervals.

    Predictive Maintenance (Condition-Based)

    Valves are serviced when diagnostic data indicates deterioration has reached an action threshold — before failure occurs but without unnecessary early intervention.

    Tool: DVC6200 friction trending. A valve with friction at 110% of baseline is fine; at 200% it's worth watching; at 350% it needs repacking before it begins to oscillate.

    Result: Fewer total maintenance interventions; no unexpected failures; right part, right time, planned labor.

    Reliability-Centered Maintenance (RCM)

    A formal methodology that identifies the failure modes of each asset, the consequence of each failure, and the optimal maintenance strategy for each failure mode. RCM selects from: - Condition monitoring (predictive) - Scheduled inspection (preventive)
    - Run-to-failure (reactive) - Design change (eliminate the failure mode)

    RCM produces the optimum maintenance plan but requires significant engineering effort. Most plants apply simplified RCM principles to critical equipment.

    14.3 AMS Device Manager — The Reliability Platform

    Emerson AMS Device Manager aggregates HART and FOUNDATION Fieldbus diagnostic data from all field devices across the plant into a centralized asset management platform. For control valves, AMS provides:

    Device Health Monitoring

    Every DVC6200 in the plant reports its status continuously:

    Status Meaning Action required
    Good / Active Device functioning normally; no alerts None
    Advisory Minor issue detected; monitor Schedule inspection
    Maintenance Significant alert; degradation confirmed Plan maintenance within 30–60 days
    Failed Device reporting fault; potential loss of control Immediate response

    The AMS dashboard shows all devices color-coded by status. A maintenance technician looking at 200 valves can see at a glance which ones need attention — without visiting a single device in the field.

    Alert Types for DVC6200

    Alert Trigger What it means
    High friction Friction exceeds user-set threshold Packing wear; lubrication needed; mechanical binding
    Travel deviation Actual position deviates from commanded Positioner can't overcome friction; actuator pressure issue
    Low supply pressure Instrument air supply drops below minimum Air supply problem; regulator failure
    Drive signal saturation Positioner driving at maximum output continuously Valve stuck; supply failure; spring broken
    Stroke deviation Valve didn't complete full stroke Mechanical obstruction; actuator spring failure
    Partial stroke failure PST travel not achieved Safety valve mechanical problem

    Historical Trending

    AMS stores the complete history of every diagnostic parameter for every device. A friction trend for a single valve over 3 years allows: - Identification of the friction increase rate (months per doubling) - Prediction of when friction will reach the action threshold - Comparison to similar valves to identify outliers - Post-repair confirmation that friction returned to baseline

    14.4 AMS Device Manager — Step-by-Step Workflow

    Understanding the actual workflow from field device to maintenance work order makes the AMS value proposition concrete for both salespeople and customers.

    Step 1: HART Polling

    AMS Device Manager communicates with each DVC6200 in the plant over the existing 4–20 mA HART loop. It does not require additional wiring — the HART signal rides on the same two wires that carry the control signal. AMS polls each device continuously (every 15–30 seconds for active alerts; every 5–15 minutes for trending data).

    Infrastructure options: - HART Multiplexer (MUX): Wired HART MUX aggregates up to 16 HART loops per card, connected to the AMS server via Ethernet. Cost-effective for groups of valves in a control room or marshalling cabinet. - WirelessHART Adapter: For remote valves or retrofits without accessible HART infrastructure, Rosemount 702/703/704 WirelessHART adapters add wireless communication to any HART device. Data reaches AMS through the Smart Wireless Gateway 1420 — no new wiring. - DeltaV integration: When DeltaV DCS is present, DeltaV passes HART data from its I/O subsystem directly to AMS via the plant Ethernet network. Zero additional hardware.

    Step 2: Alert Generation and Routing

    When a DVC6200 friction reading exceeds the configured threshold, AMS generates a timestamped alert:

    Device: FV-101 (Feed Flow Control Valve)
Alert: High Friction — Level: Maintenance Required
Friction: 3.8 times baseline (baseline 22 PSIG; current 84 PSIG)
Trend: Increasing — 0.9 PSIG/week over last 12 weeks
Recommended action: Schedule packing inspection within 60 days
Alert created: 2024-11-15 14:22:07

    AMS routes this alert to: - Email distribution list (maintenance supervisor, reliability engineer) - CMMS integration (optional — generates work order in SAP PM, IBM Maximo, or equivalent) - DeltaV operator display (HART status mapped to DCS alarm) - Applied Control monitoring dashboard (if enrolled in the Applied Control health program)

    Step 3: Alert Triage

    The maintenance reliability engineer reviews the alert in AMS context: - Trend history: Is friction rising steadily (packing wear) or was it a sudden jump (foreign object, damage)? - Loop criticality: Is this a primary control valve on a critical service, or a bypass with a spare? - Process schedule: When is the next planned outage where this valve could be serviced?

    AMS provides all of this context in the Device Health Summary view.

    Step 4: Work Order and Parts

    Based on the triage, the maintenance planner creates a work order. AMS provides: - Device record: Exact Fisher model number, body size, trim type, packing style, actuator model - Parts list: AMS links to the Fisher parts list for that exact valve assembly — the planner orders the correct packing kit, O-rings, and spare parts before the work order executes - Procedure: AMS stores and links maintenance procedures — the technician has step-by-step instructions for repacking that specific valve body style

    Step 5: Repair and Post-Maintenance Verification

    After repair, the technician uses a HART Field Communicator (375/475) or AMS to run a new valve signature. AMS stores the post-repair signature alongside the pre-repair signature. The report shows: - Pre-repair friction: 84 PSIG - Post-repair friction: 21 PSIG (close to original baseline) - Spring condition: normal - Seat condition: no change

    This documentation satisfies OSHA PSM Mechanical Integrity requirements for critical control valves — the plant has a documented record of condition assessment and corrective action.

    14.5 AMS Machinery Health Manager — Rotating Equipment

    AMS Machinery Health Manager extends the same predictive maintenance philosophy from control valves to rotating equipment: pumps, compressors, fans, gearboxes, and motors.

    CSI 2140 Portable Vibration Analyzer

    The CSI 2140 is Emerson's field vibration analyzer for route-based condition monitoring:

    Feature Specification
    Measurement 2-channel simultaneous vibration (velocity, acceleration, displacement)
    Frequency range 0.1 Hz to 80 kHz
    Dynamic range 90 dB
    Analysis FFT spectrum, time waveform, phase, cross-channel
    Database Stores measurement routes; uploads to AMS Machinery Health via USB or wireless
    Weight 1.1 kg (2.5 lb) — handheld

    Failure Mode Detection

    The CSI 2140 and Machinery Health Manager identify rotating equipment failures months before failure through characteristic vibration signatures:

    Failure Mode Vibration Signature Typical Lead Time
    Bearing inner race fault High-frequency impact at inner race frequency 2–4 months
    Bearing outer race fault Periodic impact at outer race frequency 2–6 months
    Unbalance 1× running speed, steady phase Months to years
    Misalignment 2× and axial vibration Weeks to months
    Looseness Multiple harmonics of running speed Weeks to months
    Gear tooth fault Gear mesh frequency sidebands 1–4 months
    Impeller cavitation Broadband noise + sub-synchronous Days to weeks

    Integration with AMS

    CSI 2140 route data uploads to AMS Machinery Health Manager, which: - Trends vibration amplitudes at key frequencies over time - Compares to alert levels (ISO 10816 alarm levels or user-defined) - Generates predictive maintenance work orders when amplitude exceeds alarm - Tracks maintenance history and post-repair verification data

    Dollar Impact

    A chemical plant implemented AMS Machinery Health Manager on 85 pumps and compressors. Over 3 years: detected 14 imminent failures before they became unplanned shutdowns. Average cost per avoided failure: $65,000 (combined repair + downtime). Total savings: $910,000. Program cost: $180,000 (equipment + technician time). Net 3-year benefit: $730,000.

    Field Story

Amplitude was 0.35 in/sec and rising. The gearbox was scheduled for inspection at the next opportunity — three months later, inspectors found a chipped tooth on the second stage helical gear.     14.6 The Applied Control Valve Health Program

    Applied Control offers a structured valve health program that converts the DVC6200+AMS diagnostic capability into a managed service:

    Phase 1: Baseline

    Objective: Establish the mechanical baseline for every critical valve.

    Activities: - DVC6200 installation (if not present) or verification of existing DVC6200 configuration - Full valve signature for each critical valve (offline preferred; online where possible) - AMS configuration: device tags, alert thresholds, reporting structure - Baseline report: friction level, signature shape, spring condition for each valve

    Deliverable: Baseline report with signature for every valve; alert thresholds set per application criticality.

    Phase 2: Ongoing Monitoring

    Objective: Continuous health visibility between turnarounds.

    Activities: - AMS alerts routed to Applied Control and/or plant maintenance system - Monthly review of friction trends and alert history - Quarterly summary report: valve population health status, trending concerns, recommended actions - Alert response: when a valve crosses the maintenance threshold, Applied Control schedules the repair

    Deliverable: Monthly/quarterly health reports; prioritized maintenance recommendations.

    Phase 3: Turnaround Support

    Objective: Use diagnostic data to plan turnaround work scope accurately.

    Activities: - Pre-turnaround review: which valves need repair based on friction trends and alert history - Applied Control valve shop: pull, inspect, recondition, repack, and test all flagged valves - Post-turnaround baselines: new signatures after repair confirm work quality - PRV certification: simultaneous PRV testing during turnaround window

    Deliverable: Precise turnaround scope (no over-maintenance, no missed maintenance); post-repair baseline signatures; PRV test certificates.

    14.7 Valve Diagnostic Interpretation — Case Studies

    Case 1: Rising Friction Trend

    Data: DVC6200 friction trend on a 2" Fisher ED globe valve controlling feedwater flow. Baseline friction 18 PSIG (2018). Current friction 61 PSIG (2023). Trend rate: ~9 PSIG/year.

    Interpretation: Packing is wearing — friction has tripled over 5 years. At the current rate, friction will exceed the AMS alert threshold (75 PSIG) in approximately 18 months. The valve is likely in the beginning of increased travel deviation during process upsets.

    Recommendation: Schedule packing replacement during the next planned outage (12 months out). Do not wait for failure — at 5× baseline friction, the valve will begin to affect loop performance noticeably.

    Applied Control action: Schedule shop repair; order packing kit for the ED body; plan a 4-hour maintenance window.

    Case 2: Sudden Friction Change

    Data: DVC6200 friction alert on a 4" Fisher EZ control valve — friction jumped from 22 PSIG to 145 PSIG overnight. No gradual trend.

    Interpretation: A sudden friction change is not packing wear — packing wear is gradual. This pattern indicates: foreign object in valve body; process solid accumulation in seat area; trim damage from cavitation event; packing extrusion (packing has been over-tightened or thermally damaged).

    Recommendation: Immediate inspection. Pull valve; inspect body cavity and trim for debris or damage. Do not continue operating with 145 PSIG friction — the valve is likely not responding correctly to control signals.

    Case 3: Valve Signature Shows Diaphragm Leak

    Data: Valve signature on a Fisher 657 actuator/DVC6200 assembly shows: during the pressure hold test portion of the signature, actuator pressure drops 8 PSIG over 60 seconds without any stem movement.

    Interpretation: The actuator is leaking air — either through the diaphragm or through the upper housing seal. A 8 PSIG drop in 60 seconds is significant enough to affect control in high-friction applications and will worsen.

    Recommendation: Replace actuator diaphragm. This is a 2-hour field repair — Applied Control technician on site with the diaphragm kit.

    Case 4: Spring Degradation

    Data: Valve signature slope analysis: the actuator pressure vs. travel slope has flattened — it takes 12 PSIG to compress the spring at zero travel but the spring rate shows only 0.08 PSIG/% travel vs. 0.18 PSIG/% travel at baseline.

    Interpretation: The actuator spring has fatigued or broken. A degraded spring means the fail-safe force is reduced — if instrument air fails, the valve may not fully open or close as designed.

    Recommendation: Immediate replacement. For a fail-closed valve (safety application), reduced spring force is a safety finding. For a fail-open cooling valve, reduced spring force may prevent full opening on air failure — loss of cooling.

    14.8 Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) and Valve Reliability Analysis

    MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures) is the average time between failures for a repairable asset:

    $$\text{MTBF} = \frac{\text{Total operating hours}}{\text{Number of failures}}$$

    Fisher control valves in typical process service have MTBFs ranging from: - 5–8 years: Standard service (ambient temperature, clean fluid, moderate cycling) - 3–5 years: Severe service (cavitation, high ΔP, corrosive fluid) - 1–3 years: Very severe service (extreme temperature, abrasive slurry, corrosive with high velocity)

    DVC6200 diagnostic impact on MTBF: Plants with active friction monitoring and predictive maintenance programs report 40–60% improvement in MTBF for their critical valve population — not because the valves wear less, but because interventions happen at the right time, preventing failures.

    Chapter Summary

    Reactive maintenance is the most expensive long-term strategy. Predictive maintenance — driven by DVC6200 friction trending and AMS Device Manager alerts — enables intervention at the optimal time: before failure, but not unnecessarily early. The Applied Control valve health program creates a structured relationship: baseline signatures, continuous monitoring, quarterly reports, and turnaround-scoped maintenance recommendations. The economic case is clear: one avoided unplanned shutdown at a typical plant pays for an entire year's DVC6200 monitoring program. Applied Control's combination of diagnostic software (AMS), hardware expertise (DVC6200 configuration), and repair capability (National Board Certified valve shop) creates a complete closed-loop reliability service that no other vendor in the Applied Control territory can fully replicate.

    Which maintenance strategy produces the lowest total cost over the life of an asset?

    • A) Reactive maintenance

    • D) No maintenance (run-to-failure on all assets)

    Question 2 — Analyze

    A DVC6200 friction trend shows friction at 280% of baseline. The plant maintenance manager says 'the valve is still working, I'll wait until the turnaround in 14 months.' What is your response?

    • A) At 280% of baseline friction, the valve is likely already showing travel deviation and affecting loop control quality. At the current trend rate, it will likely fail to respond to control signals within 6–8 months — well before the 14-month turnaround. A 2-hour packing replacement now prevents a potentially $50,000+ unplanned shutdown. Let me show you the friction trend chart and calculate the risk.
    • B) The manager is right — 280% is within normal operating range
    • C) Shut the valve down immediately
    • D) Increase the AMS alert threshold to 400%
