    Chapter 1: Process Control Fundamentals

    Learning Objectives

    By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

    • Remember: Define process control, identify the four elements of a control loop, and name the three modes of a PID controller
    • Understand: Explain why control is needed in industrial processes and how feedback eliminates offset
    • Apply: Trace a complete control loop in a simple process (e.g., level control on a tank) and identify each element's role
    • Analyze: Distinguish between feedback and feedforward control and determine when each is appropriate

    1.1 Why Process Control Exists

    Every industrial process — a refinery distillation column, a power plant boiler, a pharmaceutical bioreactor — has variables that must stay within defined bounds for the process to produce the right product safely and economically. Temperature too high: catalyst degrades. Pressure too low: vapor pockets form. Flow unsteady: product quality wanders.

    Left alone, processes drift. Ambient temperature changes. Feed composition fluctuates. Equipment ages. Process control is the engineering discipline that fights this drift: it continuously measures what's happening, compares it to what should happen, and makes corrections — automatically, without a human adjusting a valve by hand thousands of times per shift.

    The final element that makes the correction physical — that translates a control signal into a change in flow — is almost always a valve. This is why Applied Control's portfolio is at the center of every process control system: our products are the hands that actually move.

    1.2 The Four Elements of a Control Loop

    Every feedback control loop contains four essential elements:

        ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                                                      │
    ▼                                                      │
[PROCESS] ──measured variable──► [SENSOR/TRANSMITTER]      │
                                          │                │
                                    process variable (PV)  │
                                          │                │
                                          ▼                │
                              [CONTROLLER] ◄── set point   │
                                          │                │
                                  control output (CO)       │
                                          │                │
                                          ▼                │
                              [FINAL ELEMENT / VALVE] ──────┘
                              (manipulated variable)
    Element What it does Applied Control product
    Process The system being controlled (reactor, tank, pipe) —
    Sensor/Transmitter Measures the process variable (flow, pressure, temp, level) Rosemount transmitters
    Controller Compares PV to setpoint, calculates correction DeltaV DCS
    Final Element (Valve) Physically changes the manipulated variable (flow) Fisher control valves

    The valve is the last mile

    A control loop is only as good as its final element. A sticky packing, an oversized valve, or a positioner with excessive deadband will defeat even the best controller tuning. This is why valve diagnostics matter — and why FIELDVUE positioners with online diagnostics change the game.

    1.3 Control Variables — What We Measure and What We Manipulate

    In any loop, we need to be clear about two things:

    • Controlled variable (CV): What we want to keep at setpoint (e.g., tank level, reactor temperature, pipeline pressure)
    • Manipulated variable (MV): What we adjust to control it (e.g., the flow through the feed valve, the steam flow to the heat exchanger)

    The art of process control design is choosing the right manipulated variable — the one that has a fast, direct, and large enough effect on the controlled variable.

    Boiler drum level control

    CV: Drum water level (measured by DP transmitter or radar gauge) MV: Feed water flow rate (manipulated by a feed water control valve — typically a Fisher ES or HP series valve for high-pressure steam service) Disturbance: Steam demand fluctuations from turbines downstream

    1.4 Feedback Control — The Core Idea

    Feedback control acts after the process variable has deviated from setpoint. The controller sees the deviation and corrects it.

    The error signal drives everything:

    $$e(t) = SP - PV$$

    Where SP is the setpoint and PV is the measured process variable. The controller's job is to drive e(t) toward zero.

    Advantages: - Works even if we don't know exactly why a disturbance occurred - Self-correcting by nature - Handles unmeasured disturbances

    Limitation: - Reacts after the fact — there will always be some deviation before correction

    1.5 The PID Controller — The Workhorse of Process Control

    The PID controller is the most common controller in industry. It combines three modes of action:

    Proportional (P)

    The controller output is proportional to the current error. Larger error → bigger correction.

    $$CO_P = K_P \cdot e(t)$$

    Problem: P-only control leaves a steady-state offset — the error never quite reaches zero because when error = 0, the correction = 0 too, and the process starts drifting again.

    Integral (I)

    The controller output is proportional to the accumulated error over time. If a small error persists, integral action slowly adds correction until it's eliminated.

    $$CO_I = K_I \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau$$

    Problem: Integral action is slow and can cause integral windup (overshoot) if the valve saturates.

    Derivative (D)

    The controller output is proportional to the rate of change of the error. It anticipates where the process is headed and pre-corrects.

    $$CO_D = K_D \frac{de(t)}{dt}$$

    Problem: Derivative action amplifies sensor noise. Often set to zero for noisy measurements.

    Together:

    $$CO(t) = K_P \left[ e(t) + \frac{1}{T_I}\int e\,dt + T_D \frac{de}{dt} \right]$$

    MicroSim: Adjust P, I, and D gains and watch the process respond. Try the presets to see oscillation, sluggish, and well-tuned responses.

    1.6 Feedforward Control — Acting Before the Fact

    Feedforward control measures a disturbance and corrects for it before it affects the controlled variable. It's faster than feedback but requires knowing the disturbance in advance.

    Feedforward in a heat exchanger

    If feed flow rate to a heat exchanger increases (disturbance), a feedforward controller immediately opens the steam valve to add more heat — before the outlet temperature even drops. Feedback control then trims any remaining error.

    Fisher valve application: The steam control valve must respond quickly. A FIELDVUE DVC6200 positioner with high-performance tuning and a volume booster gives the fast stroke speeds feedforward control demands.

    1.7 Cascade Control — Controller Stacking

    In cascade control, the output of an outer (primary) controller becomes the setpoint of an inner (secondary) controller. The inner loop is faster and handles disturbances that would slow the outer loop's response.

    [Primary Controller] ──setpoint──► [Secondary Controller] ──output──► [Valve]
     (slow loop)                        (fast loop)

    Reactor temperature cascade

    • Primary (outer) loop: Reactor temperature → controls setpoint of steam flow
    • Secondary (inner) loop: Steam flow → directly controls the steam valve position
    • Benefit: If steam header pressure drops, the inner loop instantly compensates before the reactor temperature even notices

    1.9 Key Terms

    Term Definition
    Process Variable (PV) The measured quantity to be controlled (e.g., level, pressure, temperature, flow)
    Setpoint (SP) The desired value of the process variable
    Error The difference between setpoint and process variable: e = SP − PV
    Manipulated Variable (MV) The quantity adjusted by the controller to bring PV to SP
    Final Element The physical device that changes the MV; almost always a control valve
    Dead Time The delay between a change in MV and the first detectable response in PV
    Offset A persistent difference between SP and PV in proportional-only control
    Windup Integral accumulation when the controller output is saturated (valve at limits)
    Cascade A control structure where the output of one controller sets the SP of another

    Chapter Summary

    Process control exists because industrial processes drift without correction. Every feedback loop has four elements: process, sensor, controller, and final element (valve). The PID controller eliminates error using proportional response (speed), integral action (accuracy), and derivative action (anticipation). Feedforward control adds disturbance rejection speed. Cascade control handles multi-variable processes by nesting faster inner loops. Applied Control provides the final element (Fisher valves) and the control infrastructure (DeltaV DCS) that make these strategies real.

    Question 2 — Understand

    A proportional-only (P-only) controller is used on a flow control loop. After a setpoint change, the flow settles at 96% of setpoint and stays there permanently. What is the most likely cause?

    • A) The valve has too much friction
    • B) Steady-state offset from P-only control — integral action is needed
    • C) The valve is oversized
    • D) The transmitter is out of calibration

    Question 3 — Apply

    A customer has a heat exchanger where outlet temperature is too slow to respond to feed flow changes. Which control strategy would most directly improve response speed?

    • A) Increase proportional gain on the temperature controller
    • B) Add derivative action to the temperature controller
    • C) Add feedforward control from feed flow measurement to the steam valve
    • D) Switch to a smaller valve to improve rangeability
